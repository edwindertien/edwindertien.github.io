# Social Robot Design — Portfolio Assessment Rubric
**Course:** Social Robot Design (SRD)
**Total points:** 100
**Grading:** EU 1–10 = 1 + 9 × (earned / 100)

---

## How to read this rubric

Each section covers one portfolio chapter. Within each section, criteria are
grouped as follows:

- **Visualisation** — sketches, photos, diagrams, and annotated images showing
  conceptualisation, realisation, tool-in-action, and results. This replaces
  written description wherever a visual communicates better.
- **Content** — the specific knowledge, tool design, and application expected
  for that topic.
- **HRI grounding** — explicit connection to relevant literature (at least 2
  publications per section, ideally including one from an adjacent field).
- **Reflection** — what you learned, what changed in your design, and what you
  would do differently. Reflection must be specific to this topic — generic
  statements score zero.

The **in-session tool sections** (Expressiveness, Ethics/EduLARP) have a
different structure: the tool was provided by the course, so you are assessed
on the quality of your observation, your use of the tool, and your critical
reflection on its applicability — not on tool design.

The **in-depth group assignment** (section 8) asks groups to choose one design
direction — behaviour, expression, embodiment, scenario design, or ethics
tooling — and develop it to a functional or physical outcome using the actual
robot. The behaviour design tool (section 7) is always assessed independently
as a course deliverable, regardless of which direction the group chooses.

The **poster and movie** (section 9) are group deliverables assessed with a
simple 10-point checklist. Both must also appear in the individual portfolio
with a brief individual reflection on your contribution and what you would
change.

## 1. General Aspects — 10 points

*The portfolio as a document: structure, style, visual quality, referencing,
and evidence of development across the course.*

### 1.1 Structure and navigation — 2 pt
Clear chapter structure matching the course topics. A table of contents or
overview page. Sections are easy to find. Pages are well-chunked — no walls
of text.

### 1.2 Academic style — 2 pt
Complete sentences throughout. No informal or second-person language. Course
terminology used correctly and consistently. Claims are supported, not just
stated.

### 1.3 Images with captions, referenced in text — 2 pt
Every image has a descriptive caption. Images are referenced where discussed
in the running text. No decorative or unexplained images. Images carry meaning
and are not interchangeable with any other image.

### 1.4 References — literature and internal cross-references — 2 pt
Literature is cited consistently and in a recognised format. Sections
cross-reference each other where relevant — the case description is linked
from every tool section; the in-depth group work references the relevant
course tool section.

### 1.5 Portfolio coherence and learning arc — 2 pt
The portfolio reads as a developed document, not a collection of separate
assignments. There is visible evidence that later sections were informed by
earlier ones. At least one explicit moment where you reflect on how your
understanding of the design case changed across the course.

## 2. Case Description — 10 points

*The design case is the thread running through the whole portfolio. It must be
specific, grounded, and developed — not a hypothetical.*

### 2.1 Case explanation and target audience — 2 pt
The deployment context, the robot's role, and the direct and indirect user
groups are described clearly and specifically. Not "a hospital" but "the
outpatient waiting room of the physiotherapy department at [institution]."
The case is real or realistic enough to have been discussed with someone in
that context.

### 2.2 Visualisation — 2 pt
The case is visualised: context photos or sketches, stakeholder diagrams,
scenario thumbnails, or a visual overview of the design space. A reader who
skips the text should still understand what the case is about.

### 2.3 Core HRI literature — 3 pt
At least 5 publications directly relevant to the case are cited and discussed.
"Discussed" means each publication is connected to a specific design decision
or open question in the case — not a bibliography entry with a one-line
summary.

### 2.4 Adjacent field literature — 3 pt
At least 5 publications from adjacent fields (e.g. service design, medical
communication, educational psychology, human factors) are cited and discussed.
These should expand the design space, not just confirm the HRI framing.

## 3. Scenario Design Tool — 10 points

*You designed and applied a scenario-based design tool for your case.*

### 3.1 Visualisation — 2 pt
Visualise: the tool's structure (cards, canvas, protocol), at least one moment
of the tool in action, and the output it produced. Captions label each stage:
conceptualisation, realisation, tool-in-action, result.

### 3.2 Tool design — HRI knowledge embedded — 3 pt
The tool is grounded in at least one theoretical framework from the course
(scenario-based design, narrative templates, boundary condition analysis, or
similar). Make explicit what HRI-specific knowledge the tool embeds and what
it enables that a generic scenario tool would not.

### 3.3 Application to your case — 3 pt
Document the session: describe the experiment, the observations, and the
output. Quote or reproduce something the tool produced. What design decision
did using the tool inform or change?

### 3.4 Evaluation and reflection — 2 pt
Evaluate on two axes: (a) outcome quality — did the tool produce a better
design decision than you would have reached without it? (b) tool quality —
was it specific, usable, and generalisable? What would you change? Connect to
at least one relevant publication.

## 4. Expressiveness Design Tool — 8 points

*This session used a course-provided tool (the Laban-based motion vocabulary
workshop / expressive arms session). You are assessed on observation, critical
use, and reflection — not on tool design.*

### 4.1 Visualisation — 2 pt
Visualise what you observed and what you produced: annotated motion sketches,
Laban effort/shape notation, photographs of expressive arm positions or
sequences, or video stills with annotation. Detailed enough that someone not
in the session can understand what happened.

### 4.2 Structured observation — 3 pt
Describe the session using a structured framework: what the tool asked you to
do, what the robot or arm produced, what observers perceived. Go beyond
describing what happened: what did the tool make visible that was not visible
before? Where did the tool's vocabulary reach its limits for your case?

### 4.3 Reflection — applicability and HRI grounding — 3 pt
Critically assess: which aspects of the tool apply directly to your case, and
which do not? What would need to change? Ground the reflection in at least one
publication on non-verbal robot communication, Laban movement analysis, or
expressive HRI.

## 5. Embodiment Design Tool — 10 points

*You designed and applied a tool for making embodiment decisions for your case
(AI-assisted, collection-based, or another approach).*

### 5.1 Visualisation — 2 pt
Visualise: the tool's structure and materials, the embodiment options it
generated or compared, at least one moment of use, and the design outcome.
Label each image: conceptualisation, realisation, in-action, result.

### 5.2 Tool design — HRI knowledge embedded — 3 pt
What HRI-specific knowledge is built into the tool? This might include
proxemics, form/function mapping, the Uncanny Valley, or the relationship
between embodiment and perceived capability. Make explicit how the tool
operationalises this — not just "the tool considers proxemics" but how the
tool structure forces or guides a proxemics-aware decision.

### 5.3 Application to your case — 3 pt
Document the session: inputs required, options generated or structured,
observations made, and the embodiment direction reached. What changed in your
case description as a result?

### 5.4 Evaluation and reflection — 2 pt
Evaluate outcome and tool quality. What would you change? Connect to at least
one publication on robot embodiment, morphological design, or human-robot
proxemics.

## 6. Ethics / EduLARP — 6 points

*This session used a course-provided tool (the EduLARP ethics scenario
experience). You are assessed on observation and reflection — not on tool
design.*

### 6.1 Visualisation — 1 pt
Document the session visually: photos, a sketch of the scenario setup, or
annotated notes from the roleplay. At minimum one image with a caption
explaining the ethical situation being explored.

### 6.2 Structured observation — 2 pt
Describe what happened using a structured approach: which ethical tensions were
made visible, how different stakeholders experienced the robot's behaviour,
and what the roleplay format revealed that a desk-based analysis would not.
Specific and concrete — not a general statement about ethics being important.

### 6.3 Reflection — applicability and HRI grounding — 3 pt
Critically reflect: which ethical issues surfaced are relevant to your design
case? What would an adapted version of this tool look like for your context?
Ground the reflection in at least one publication on robot ethics,
value-sensitive design, or care robotics ethics.

## 7. Behaviour Design Tool — 10 points

*You designed and applied a high-level software design discussion tool for
robot behaviour. This may operate at an abstract level (architecture diagrams,
state machines, decision trees) but must have been tested in at least a brief
WoZ session or functional prototype.*

### 7.1 Visualisation — 2 pt
Visualise: the tool's structure, the architecture or behaviour model it
produced for your case, and at least one observation from testing. Label
each: conceptualisation, realisation, in-action, result.

### 7.2 Tool design — HRI knowledge embedded — 3 pt
The tool must operationalise at least one HRI-relevant concept: reactive vs.
deliberative behaviour, autonomy allocation, graceful degradation, or social
signal interpretation. Make explicit how the tool embeds this knowledge and
what it enables that a generic software design tool would not.

### 7.3 Application to your case — 3 pt
Document the session: what did the tool produce, how was it tested (WoZ,
simulation, or functional prototype), and what did you observe? What did
testing reveal that the design-time tool did not anticipate?

### 7.4 Evaluation and reflection — 2 pt
Evaluate outcome and tool quality. What is the relationship between this tool
and your group assignment direction? If your group chose a different direction,
explain briefly how this work informed that choice. Connect to at least one
publication on robot control architectures or behaviour-based robotics.

## 8. In-Depth Tool Design — Group Assignment — 26 points

*Groups choose one design direction and develop it to a functional or physical
outcome using the actual robot hardware. Directions: behaviour (autonomous
programming), expression (behaviour or puppeteering), embodiment
(physical/material), scenario design (interactive testing), or ethics tooling
(structured stakeholder engagement). The choice must be motivated.*

### 8.1 Motivated direction choice — 4 pt
State clearly which direction you chose and why. The motivation must connect
to your case: which aspect of your design case most needs a better tool, and
why is the chosen direction the right one to address it? If you are building
on a course tool section, explain what you are extending and why. If you chose
a different direction, explain the gap the course tool did not fill.

### 8.2 Visualisation — conceptualisation and physicalisation — 6 pt
Document the design process in two phases:

- **Conceptualisation:** sketches, diagrams, architecture drawings, or card
  designs showing how the tool was conceived and iterated before building.
- **Physicalisation:** photos, video stills, or annotated screenshots showing
  the tool being built, programmed, or assembled. Show intermediate states,
  not just the final version.

### 8.3 Test plan and method — 3 pt
Describe the test plan: who tested the tool, in what setting, with what
protocol, and observing or measuring what. The test must involve the actual
hardware or a realistic proxy. If the plan changed during execution, document
what changed and why.

### 8.4 Observations — tool in action — 6 pt
Document what happened during testing: structured observations, unexpected
findings, moments of failure or success, and direct quotes or measurements
where available. Show evidence the test happened — photographs, logs, or
observer notes.

### 8.5 Resulting redesign — 2 pt
Based on observations, describe what changed in the tool or the design. The
iteration step must be visible: the result of testing should appear in the
final version of the tool or in an explicit design decision for the case.

### 8.6 Evaluation and reflection — 5 pt
Evaluate on two axes:

- **Design outcome:** is the resulting robot behaviour, expression, embodiment,
  or scenario design better, more grounded, or more justified than before?
- **Tool quality:** is the tool specific enough to be constraining, usable
  enough for someone outside the team, and generalisable to another case?
  Would you recommend it? What would need to change?

Ground the reflection in at least 2 relevant publications.

## 9. Poster and Movie — 10 points

*Group deliverable, assessed with a binary checklist: each criterion is met
(1 pt) or not met (0 pt). Both the poster and the movie must appear in the
individual portfolio with a brief individual reflection on your contribution
and what you would change.*

### Poster — 5 points

| # | Criterion |
|---|---|
| P1 | **Case and tool identifiable in under 10 seconds** — a reader who has never seen the project can determine what the robot, the context, and the tool are without reading the body text |
| P2 | **Tool structure is visualised** — the tool's logic, protocol, or components appear as a diagram, canvas, or annotated image, not described in prose |
| P3 | **Evidence of application is shown** — at least one image or diagram showing the tool in use with the robot or in the deployment context |
| P4 | **Result is stated explicitly** — the design outcome is visible as a clearly labelled element, not buried in text |
| P5 | **Production quality** — layout is clean and readable; font sizes are legible at poster distance; visual hierarchy guides the eye from problem to tool to result |

### Movie — 5 points

| # | Criterion |
|---|---|
| M1 | **Challenge framed in the first 30 seconds** — the viewer knows what design problem the group tackled and why it matters before any tool description begins |
| M2 | **Tool is demonstrated, not just described** — the movie shows the tool in use with the actual hardware or deployment context, not a slide deck about the tool |
| M3 | **At least one failure or unexpected finding is included** — the movie acknowledges something that did not work as expected and what the team did with that |
| M4 | **Evaluation speaks to both design outcome and tool quality** — two distinct verdicts: did the robot design improve, and does the tool work as a tool? |
| M5 | **Production quality** — audio is clear and intelligible throughout; visual material is stable and well-framed; the 5-minute format is respected |
